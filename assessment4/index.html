COMM2749 · Digital Media Studio 1 · 2026

From fragments to worlds.

Over twelve weeks, I moved from editing moments into a self-portrait, to designing a responsive system of icons, to building a space a player could move through. This is a record of how I learned to make meaning through sequence, interaction and place.

01 / Starting point

I came to make things, not just analyse them.

I began this program after studying Data Science. I already knew how to work with editing software; what I wanted to understand was how to choose a form because it could say something.

I am drawn to games, digital culture, illustration and virtual environments. I joined the course in Week 3, so my first stretch was also a catch-up: I had no draft to share yet and needed to turn scattered ideas into a workable plan. Storyboarding helped me find a direction, and sound design showed me that a transition can carry meaning even when two images do not belong to the same time or place.

Across the semester, my question shifted: first, how can images connect parts of a life? Then, how can a visual system invite a response? Finally, how can a designed space make a player feel a change by moving through it?

Project 01 · Video self-portrait
01

Campus Life

A two-minute portrait assembled from everyday fragments: friends, study, drawing, city details and quieter observations of Melbourne.

Studio1_Assignment102:08 · 2026

What I chose to show

The campus, my friends, drawing and an ACG merchandise shop each show a different part of my life. I wanted quieter moments alongside social ones, because spending time alone also belongs in this portrait. Drawing connects my everyday habits with the hope of doing work I enjoy in the future.

I filmed drawing near desk height to make it feel quiet and close, and campus at walking height to share my viewpoint. Warm-looking locations and space around the subject kept the images relaxed. I wanted these qualities to come from the footage itself.

Sound sometimes suggests an inner feeling while the image shows an ordinary activity. Letting it begin before a cut or linger afterwards connects different places, like a memory continuing into the next moment. Longer shots at emotional pauses, especially after quicker cuts, give the edit room to breathe. I learned to follow the feeling of a moment as well as what happened.

Project 02 · Responsive icon system

System

Nine flat, unified icons use chess-piece forms to explore how a system can communicate through visual relationships and responsive feedback.

Finding a shared language

My apple and chess thumbnails explored different directions. Chess gave me a stronger connection between the symbols: each piece has a different role within the same system. Flattening the sketches made their edges and scale more consistent, although some of the loose character of the drawing disappeared.

I compared monochromatic, analogous, complementary and triadic palettes. The finished page uses cream on charcoal, with violet for the active state. Keeping that change consistent across the set felt clearer than giving every icon a different colour. The preview here follows the folio’s monochrome treatment; the original page keeps the cream and violet palette.

Each icon has an individual sound and alternate image. The repeated interaction helps them feel related, while the sounds suggest different characters and actions. Some abstract symbols are less immediately recognisable than the chess pieces. Next time, I would compare what someone recognises at a small size before polishing every outline.

Building a place to explore

I began with a pavilion and garden corridor, with birds and wind outside. The gallery is more controlled: a geometric sculpture is fenced off and lit from below. Passing through a dark opening and dropping into a world of whale and pterosaur skeletons breaks that order. I wanted the final space to feel strange and much larger than the place before it.

In the film I controlled the frame; here the player can stop, turn back or look away. Working at eye level made scale and visibility more important than they seemed from above. Sound changes with the setting too: a ten-second rumble and fading music lead into the underwater layers.

In my friend’s recorded test, they approached the sculpture, looked up, and explored both skeletons. The main landmarks drew attention, but the recording alone cannot tell me whether the story was understood. Turning towards the edge also exposed a bright outer horizon. I would refine that boundary and the lighting so the underwater atmosphere holds up from more directions.

What the feedback changed: my tutor liked the crafted spaces and transitions, but found too little explanation of the idea and development. That made me realise that showing the final build was only part of presenting the work. The journal now explains the route, sound decisions and what the recorded test revealed.

How my decisions developed

From learning the tools to choosing the form.

The course kept bringing me back to the same practical question: what should the audience do, notice or feel next? The answer changed with the medium.

01

Storyboard

Turn a loose idea into a sequence. Decide what each shot contributes before the edit grows into a collection of attractive moments.

02

Bridge

Use rhythm and sound to connect discontinuous images. Let laughter carry into waves, then allow the quieter shot to breathe.

03

Respond

Give each icon a distinct but coherent visual and audio response. Interaction should clarify the system, not interrupt it.

04

Navigate

Shape a route through encounters. Test whether layout, scale, light and sound prepare the player for each change.

A practice still forming

What I want my work to make possible.

I am most interested when a design lets someone discover meaning at their own pace: by noticing a cut, trying a symbol, or choosing to keep walking.

That interest connects the three projects, but each one also taught me a different limit. A montage can become vague if its fragments do not share an emotional direction. An icon system can become opaque if its forms are not legible without words. A navigable environment can be beautifully made and still leave its intention unexplained if I show only the final build.

The feedback on Descent helped me see that documentation is part of the design practice. I need to make my decisions visible: what I tried, what changed, how a test affected the route, and why the final transition works the way it does. Watching the recorded playthrough made this practical: the player looked towards an outer edge that I had paid less attention to while building. Documentation helped me see a specific improvement rather than simply describe the atmosphere I wanted.

The next direction

I want to design the spaces between events.

Across the rest of the program, I plan to specialise in interactive environment design and spatial storytelling for games. I want to combine level layout, visual atmosphere, sound and interaction so that a player can understand a change by moving through it.

I want to keep developing my drawing and digital art alongside practical skills in Unity. My aim is to make environments that are visually distinctive but also readable: places where a small shift in light, sound or scale can change what the player believes about the world.

This semester began with fragments of everyday life. It ends with a more specific question I want to keep exploring: how can a designed space tell someone that they have changed?
