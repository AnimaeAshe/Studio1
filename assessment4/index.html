From fragments to worlds.

Over twelve weeks, I moved from editing moments into a self-portrait, to designing a responsive system of icons, to building a space a player could move through. This is a record of how I learned to make meaning through sequence, interaction and place.

Process journal ↗

01 / Starting point

I came to make things, not just analyse them.

I began this program after studying Data Science. I already knew how to work with editing software; what I wanted to understand was how to choose a form because it could say something.

I am drawn to games, digital culture, illustration and virtual environments. I joined the course in Week 3, so my first stretch was also a catch-up: I had no draft to share yet and needed to turn scattered ideas into a workable plan. Storyboarding helped me find a direction, and sound design showed me that a transition can carry meaning even when two images do not belong to the same time or place.

Across the semester, my question shifted: first, how can images connect parts of a life? Then, how can a visual system invite a response? Finally, how can a designed space make a player feel a change by moving through it?

Project 01 · Video self-portrait
01

Campus Life

A two-minute portrait assembled from everyday fragments: friends, study, drawing, city details and quieter observations of Melbourne.

Studio1_Assignment102:08 · 2026

What I chose to show

I chose the campus, friends, drawing and an ACG merchandise shop because these are places and activities I spend time with. The quieter shots mattered too: being alone belongs in the portrait as much as social life. Filming drawing at desk height made it feel close, while walking through campus at eye level shared my viewpoint. Warm light and space around the subject came from filming choices rather than filters.

I wanted the edit to follow how these moments felt. J-cuts and L-cuts let sound arrive early or linger after an image, so separate places could feel connected like a memory. Sometimes the image shows an ordinary activity while the sound suggests an inner feeling. Longer shots after quicker cuts gave the rhythm room to breathe.

This made me more selective about footage. An attractive shot still needed to say something about my life or help the emotional sequence. That question became useful later when choosing what belonged in the folio.

Project 02 · Responsive icon system
02

System

Nine flat, unified icons use chess-piece forms to explore how a system can communicate through visual relationships and responsive feedback.

Icon suite / 09 formsAS2
Movement inside a system.
Image swap + soundHover / click

Finding a shared language

I usually prefer spontaneous drawing, so working with a system pushed me towards a different kind of decision. Chess gave the symbols recognisable roles and relationships. Moving from thumbnails to Illustrator made the edges and proportions more consistent, but also removed some of the loose line quality I enjoy. I chose consistency because the shapes had to work together at a small size.

The colour studies compared four harmonies. For the final page, cream on charcoal keeps the normal state quiet and violet marks a response. Repeating the same change across the set gives it unity. Each icon has its own sound, but the interaction stays consistent. I began to see behaviour as part of the visual language.

Some abstract symbols are still less clear than the chess pieces. Uniform styling does not solve that. Next time I would check what someone recognises before polishing every outline. The preview here is monochrome; the original page retains the cream and violet.

Project 03 · Navigable 3D environment
03

Descent

A first-person environment moves from ordered display into a submerged, collapsing world. The transformation unfolds through architecture, light, sound and the player's route.

Building a place to explore

I began with a pavilion and garden corridor, with birds and wind outside. The gallery is more controlled: a geometric sculpture is fenced off and lit from below. Passing through a dark opening and dropping into a world of whale and pterosaur skeletons breaks that order. I wanted the final space to feel strange and much larger than the place before it.

In the film I controlled the frame; here the player can stop, turn back or look away. Working at eye level made scale and visibility more important than they seemed from above. Sound changes with the setting too: a ten-second rumble and fading music lead into the underwater layers.

My friend stopped at the sculpture and explored the skeletons in the recorded test. This showed that the landmarks drew attention, but did not prove that the story was understood. Their camera also faced a bright outer horizon I had overlooked. I would refine the boundary and lighting so the underwater feeling survives a turn away from the main view. That is a proposed improvement, not a change already made to the build.

What challenged my assumption: the environment felt clear to me because I knew its intention. My tutor liked the spaces and transitions, but found too little explanation of their development. The feedback and recorded test showed two different gaps: what I had not explained, and what I had not noticed from another viewpoint.

How my decisions developed

From learning the tools to choosing the form.

The course kept bringing me back to the same practical question: what should the audience do, notice or feel next? The answer changed with the medium.

01

Make it concrete

I often began with a mood. A storyboard or a small sketch gave me something I could arrange and change.

02

Choose what matters

A shot, colour or object needed a reason to stay. More material did not always make the idea clearer.

03

Consider the response

The icon page made me think about what happens after someone acts, as well as what they first see.

04

Look from elsewhere

The playtest showed me a view I had overlooked. Knowing the scene well had also made some problems less visible to me.

Weeks 10–12 · Making this folio

Learning what to leave out.

The folio became another design exercise. I wanted a cinematic atmosphere, but the projects and their decisions still had to be easy to find.

Selection

Fewer, more specific examples

I chose material that shows a decision: the sound plan, icon sketches, colour comparison and a view from the playtest. A small selection helps explain the work without turning the page into a screenshot archive.

A practice still forming

What I want my work to make possible.

Walking through the Unity corridor felt different from looking at it from above. I became interested in making a place someone could experience at their own pace.

The earlier projects still belong in that direction. The film taught me to think about emotional rhythm and sound. The icons made me work with consistent shapes and responses, even when I missed the freedom of drawing. In an environment, both matter: atmosphere can draw someone in, while a clear visual language helps them find their way.

Explaining the work was harder than making it look finished. I had left too many decisions to memory. Next semester I want to record small choices as I go, including ideas I drop and views that do not work. That would make it easier to compare versions and explain why something changed.

The next direction

I want to design the spaces between events.

Across the rest of the program, I plan to specialise in game environment design and spatial storytelling. I want to develop my drawing and digital art alongside Unity, bringing the film’s attention to sound and pacing together with the icons’ visual consistency.

I am especially interested in transitions: how an ordinary corridor can prepare someone for a strange space, or how a sound can change the feeling of entering it. My next step is to make those atmospheres hold up from the player’s viewpoint, including the directions I would not naturally choose to look.
